Popularity vs. Being Load-Bearing

Degree measures how many connections a character has. Betweenness measures how many shortest paths depend on them. On the Marvel character network's giant component, plotting one against the other on a log–log scale — then asking who over-performs what their own degree predicts — surfaces a character with only two connections who turns out to be structurally irreplaceable, and a handful of other genuine bridges that raw popularity alone would never reveal.

The Rockman Surprise

A log-log scatter of betweenness centrality against degree, fit with a trend line, surfaces which nodes over- or under-perform what their own degree would predict.

Discussion question

“Who is the most surprising character in the network by any centrality, against the right null? Make the case, with a figure, and tell us what they bridge.”

Answer

Rockman_(character). Degree 2 — connected only to Black_Widow_(Natasha_Romanova) and The_Witness_(character) — yet the single largest positive residual in the entire betweenness-vs-degree scatter below: +2.02, far above what any other degree-2 node reaches.

The reason: The_Witness_(character) is a degree-1 dead end, and Rockman is its only link to the rest of the network. Every shortest path from The_Witness_(character) to anyone else has to pass through him — so low degree doesn't mean low importance, if you happen to be someone's sole bridge to everywhere else.

What surprised me: betweenness centrality alone can actually overstate a node's importance. Rockman's whole score comes from one fact — Witness has no other way out — not from bridging anything large or structurally significant. Look at his actual impact on the network instead of his betweenness value: removing him leaves a giant component of 275 nodes, exactly tied with Deadpool, Deathlok, Genis-Vell, Guardsman_(character), Moon_Knight, and Nightmask — completely unremarkable, nowhere near Spider-Man (271, and it fragments into 6 pieces instead of 2). What's genuinely unusual about Rockman isn't his importance to the network — it's his efficiency: no other node gets this much betweenness from only 2 connections. The real claim this page is making is narrower, and more honest, than "most important character": Rockman is the most surprising character relative to what his own degree predicts, not the most structurally critical one.

Try It on the Real Network

The full 277-node giant component renders as an unreadable hairball, so pick a smaller, self-contained neighborhood to browse instead — computed on the fly with the same breadth-first search from earlier, just bounded to a fixed number of hops from one (or several) characters. Click any node to remove it: node size is degree (how many connections, right now — it shrinks live as you cut a node's neighbors), and node color is betweenness centrality on a sequential scale from pale lilac to deep purple — a from-scratch Brandes' algorithm running in your browser, recomputed and cached once per click rather than continuously. Faded, translucent nodes have been stranded outside the current biggest cluster.

Each view below combines two or three bridge characters' neighborhoods into one: a single character's 1-hop neighborhood alone is basically a star, where every shortest path already runs through the center and betweenness has nowhere to redistribute onto as you remove nodes. These combined views avoid that — each pair's neighborhoods overlap only at a couple of shared hub characters, so removing one bridge forces its leftover neighbors to route through whatever connection is left. Watch for a small, pale node that suddenly darkens once its neighborhood's other exit disappears.

Each pairing puts two of the bridge characters from earlier in the same view: Hercules (cosmic/Avengers-adjacent teammates), Black Widow (the spy/espionage cluster), and U.S. Agent (a cluster of lower-profile military/team-support characters) each anchor their own corner, joined to the others only through a couple of shared hub characters — Spider-Man and Wolverine show up again and again. Try Hercules + U.S. Agent and click Hercules first: Ares_(Marvel_Comics) — still just degree 3 — jumps roughly 4.7× in betweenness as soon as Hercules is gone, becoming a brand-new bridge in a network he wasn't previously load-bearing in at all. All three bridges together gives the richest version of this: three separate neighborhoods, several shared hub connectors, and the most room for betweenness to genuinely move around as you remove nodes. Rockman_(character) himself doesn't appear in any of these views — his effect depends on a degree-1 dead end (The_Witness_(character)) that none of these neighborhoods happen to include; see the figure above and the isolated toy demo below for that mechanism specifically.

Try it: the Rockman effect, isolated

A small synthetic graph: a dense 5-node hub clique, one bridge node with degree exactly 2, and a variable number of leaves hanging off the bridge. Betweenness recomputed live (Brandes' algorithm) as you drag the slider.

Leaves hanging off the bridge 6
Drag "leaves" up: the bridge's degree never changes — it always has exactly 2 neighbors, the hub side and one leaf-facing side — but its betweenness climbs because more nodes depend on it as their only route. Now tick the "back door" box: once leaves have an alternative path to the hub, the bridge's betweenness collapses toward zero even though nothing about its own degree changed. That's the Rockman effect, isolated.

Who Actually Holds the Network Together?

Betweenness predicts who carries a lot of shortest-path traffic. It doesn't automatically predict who's irreplaceable — those are different questions, and the giant-component explorer above already hinted at the gap. Two ways to test it directly: knock out one character at a time and measure the actual damage, then remove characters in bulk, by different strategies, and watch how fast the network comes apart.

The prediction holds at the very top: Spider-Man is simultaneously #1 by betweenness and the single most damaging character to remove — his 271-node aftermath (mentioned above) is the worst any single removal produces. But go past the very top and the two rankings mostly decouple (Spearman = 0.31, weak) — Hercules, betweenness rank #7, causes zero additional stranding when removed alone. Every shortest path that nominally "passed through" him has an equally-short alternate route through someone else; he carries traffic without being anyone's only road. Black Widow is the flip side: only rank #8 by betweenness, but the second-most damaging single removal in the whole network — betweenness undersells her precisely because she's a genuine cut-adjacent bridge, not just a busy one.

In bulk: targeted attack vs. random failure — compared to what?

The textbook result, reproduced first: random failure barely dents it — nearly a straight line, since most characters aren't anyone's sole route to anywhere. Both targeted strategies collapse far faster, and past ~30% removed they fall off a cliff into a handful of disconnected fragments. Betweenness and degree track each other closely (removing the biggest hub usually also removes whoever carries the most traffic) — the real daylight between the two measures is in the single-node cases above (Hercules, Black Widow), not in this bulk view.
The real network is consistently, if modestly, more fragile than its own degree sequence predicts — at every checkpoint, under both targeted strategies, the real curve sits 2–5 percentage points below the configuration-model null's mean, outside its shaded band through most of the removal range. Degree heterogeneity alone (the null, which has zero real-world structure beyond who-connects-to-how-many) already explains most of the vulnerability, same as the clustering and distance-distribution results elsewhere on this site — but not quite all of it. The remaining gap is consistent with what Part 1 above found directly: real bridges like Black Widow sit between genuine real-world clusters (spies, Spider-people, cosmic heroes) that a degree-preserving rewiring scrambles away, so the null's "important" nodes are interchangeable in a way the real network's aren't.

Do the Biggest Cliques Turn Out to Be Teams?

A clique here is a set of characters whose articles all link to each other, pairwise — the tightest possible structure a network can have. Finding the biggest ones on the giant component (893 maximal cliques in total, nx.find_cliques) and naming their members is the direct way to ask whether "team" is a real structural feature of this network, or just a label we'd like to project onto it.

The X-Men panel is the more informative one precisely because it isn't a single clique but the union of six overlapping ones: 51 of 55 possible edges are real, and all four missing pairs (Cable–Storm, and Jubilee/Northstar/Phoenix with each other) involve only the "swappable" characters — never the six-member core. Are they all teams? Essentially yes. Morbius and Werewolf by Night both carry Wikipedia's own Midnight Sons category — that cluster is a real, named team, not a coincidence. The street-level cluster doesn't share one clean Wikipedia team category (Misty Knight and Paladin have none at all), but it's the same mercenary/support-hero neighborhood U.S. Agent was already shown bridging into the hub core, earlier on this page — found again independently, this time by clique instead of betweenness. Every large clique in this network names a real cluster; none of them are structurally arbitrary.

Homophily: does sharing a team predict a link, more than chance?

Two attributes, both scraped fresh from Wikipedia's category system for every character in the giant component: team (X-Men / Guardians of the Galaxy / Fantastic Four — whichever category matched first) and first-appearance decade. Both tested the same correct way: shuffle the attribute labels across the fixed real network (200 times), not the edges — the question is whether this labeling of this real network is unusual, not whether the wiring itself is (that's a different null, used elsewhere on this site for degree and clustering).

Both are far stronger than chance — team by a lot more than decade. Team coverage is thin (Wikipedia's own team categories turn out to be genuinely inconsistent — Misty Knight and U.S. Agent, both real, well-documented heroes, carry no clean "team members" category at all, which is exactly why this section leaned on cliques first to confirm teams are real before trying to label them), but among the 34 characters a team label could be assigned to, 86% of their edges stay inside the same team, for z ≈ 10.8 against 200 attribute-label shuffles — not a close call. Decade homophily is real too (z ≈ 6.0) but far gentler (r=0.16 vs. r=0.57): sharing an origin era makes two characters modestly more likely to link, but sharing a team — literally co-starring in the same stories — predicts it far more strongly. That ordering makes narrative sense, and it's reassuring to see the numbers say so directly.

A Random Walk Is PageRank

PageRank's own definition is a random surfer: at every step, with probability α follow a uniformly random outgoing link, and with probability 1−α teleport to a uniformly random character anywhere in the network (a dead end with no outgoing links always teleports). Walk that surfer for long enough on the real directed network below, and the fraction of time it spends at each character should converge onto real PageRank — precomputed with networkx.pagerank in the week-3 notebook, validated there against a hand-written implementation to six decimal places. Watch the correlation climb as you add steps — or click any of the current character's outgoing links directly to steer the walk yourself; a manual click counts as a step too, folded into the same tally as the automatic ones.

A handful of steps land almost anywhere — most points sit far from the diagonal, and the empirical top ten barely resembles the true one. By a thousand steps the correlation is usually well past 0.9; by ten thousand, the two top-ten lists are nearly identical. Try α = 0.5: the walk teleports so often that it barely uses the link structure at all, and it still converges — just onto a flatter, less concentrated PageRank, since heavy teleportation drowns out exactly the hub-favoring effect that makes Spider-Man, Hulk, and Wolverine dominate at α = 0.85. Steering by hand (click a link in the diagram instead of Step Once) is a good way to feel the mechanism directly: try always picking the least familiar name offered — you'll drift toward the network's periphery, and the empirical leaderboard will stay far from the true one no matter how many manual steps you take, since you're systematically avoiding exactly the hubs that real PageRank (and an unbiased random walk) favor.
