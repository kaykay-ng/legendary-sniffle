📊 Binning Methods Analysis

How to Choose the Right Binning Strategy for Your Data

The Core Question

When visualizing a distribution on a log-log plot, how you divide the data into bins dramatically affects what story the visualization tells. The same underlying data can look completely different depending on your binning choice.

Key insight: Binning is not a technical detail — it's a choice about which patterns to emphasize and which to hide.

The Three Main Methods

❌ Poor for Marvel

1. Linear Binning (Equal-Width Bins)

How it works: Divides the degree range into equally-sized intervals. If degrees range 1-107 with 20 bins, each bin is ~5 degrees wide.

When to use:

  • Data uniformly distributed across its range
  • Meaningful intervals matter (e.g., age groups: 0-10, 10-20, 20-30 years)
  • Temperature ranges, salary brackets, test score ranges

For Marvel: Creates few large bins for the sparse tail, obscures dense early region. Wastes resolution where it matters most.

⚠️ Mixed for Marvel

2. Quantile Binning (Equal-Frequency Bins)

How it works: Ensures each bin has roughly the same number of observations. With 15 bins and 303 nodes, each bin has ~20 nodes.

When to use:

  • You care about tiers/percentiles
  • Comparative demographics (income quintiles: top 20%, next 20%, etc.)
  • Equal representation across groups matters

For Marvel: Would work if you want "character tiers" (top performers vs. mid tier vs. background characters), but still wastes bins on dense early region.

✅ Perfect for Marvel

3. Logarithmic Binning (Equal-Log Increments, "Goodies Recipe")

How it works: Creates bins that are equal in log space. Width-1 bins for low degrees (1-7), then doubling: [8-16), [16-32), [32-64), etc.

When to use:

  • Power-law/scale-free distributions
  • Multi-scale phenomena spanning many orders of magnitude
  • Network degree distributions, earthquake magnitudes (Gutenberg-Richter), city populations (Zipf), wealth (Pareto)

For Marvel: Automatically narrows bins where data is dense (low degrees), widens where sparse (high degrees). Each order of magnitude gets equal visual weight.

The Marvel network has a fundamental structural imbalance: most characters are weakly connected, but a few (like Spider-Man with in-degree 106) are extremely central. This mismatch between dense early region and sparse tail is exactly what logarithmic binning handles perfectly.

Linear binning's problem: Gives equal bin width to all degree ranges → wastes space on sparse tail, misses detail in dense region

Quantile binning's problem: Forces equal counts per bin → wastes many bins on dense region, leaves few wide bins for sparse tail (backwards!)

Logarithmic binning's solution: Automatically adapts bin width to data density

  • Narrow bins (1-2 degrees each) where many characters cluster (low degrees)
  • Wide bins (10-30 degrees each) where few characters exist (high degrees)
  • Power-law relationships appear as straight lines on log-log plots

The Bigger Lesson

Binning is not a technical detail you hide in your methods section. It's a visualization design choice that fundamentally changes what the data communicates:

  • Linear binning says: "I care about equal intervals"
  • Quantile binning says: "I care about equal representation"
  • Logarithmic binning says: "I want to see multi-scale structure; I suspect a power law"

Choosing a binning method = choosing what story to tell with your data. For network scientists, logarithmic binning is the default choice because networks are inherently multi-scale phenomena. But understand *why* — so you can choose differently when your data demands it.
